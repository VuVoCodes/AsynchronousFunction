# Response to Reviewer gN93 (Borderline Accept, 4)

Thank you very much for the careful and constructive review, and for recognizing PGGB as novel, lightweight, and easy to combine with existing gradient modulation approaches. The four concerns all target the right places. Below are our responses to each question, with new measurements where applicable.

**[Q1] In Section 3.5, the safety properties are described as ensuring that the method does not harm low-imbalance training. What should we expect in high-imbalance settings? Are there any stability guarantees or diagnostic criteria for when boosting may become harmful?**

**Response.** This is the right question to ask of Section 3.5, which should have made the scoping explicit. The propositions cover both regimes, in the following division of labor.

1. The descent bound (Prop. 3) is **uniform in the imbalance level**: it holds for any utilization gap, for step sizes satisfying its condition ($\eta \le 1/(L s_{\max}^2)$). It is stated for SGD (Section 3.5). Among the high-imbalance benchmarks this covers CREMA-D, where the guarantee is that PGGB preserves standard descent with gradient variance inflated by at most $s_{\max}^2 = 4$. CMU-MOSI and CMU-MOSEI train with Adam (Section 4.1) and rest on the empirical results.
2. Prop. 2 is the complementary guarantee near balance: the intervention is provably small near exact balance.
3. What is deliberately **not** guaranteed is that boosting closes the gap in high imbalance. That claim is empirical (Section 4.4: weak probe +9.41 pp, strong probe -0.81 pp, post-hoc utilization gap reduced $5.4\times$).
4. On diagnostic criteria: our operating-conditions analysis (Section 4.3.1) identifies the failure regime observed in practice, a thin data budget combined with weak inherent asymmetry, where the failure localizes to the throttling component rather than the boost. The monitored quantities $\delta$ and $\bar{s}_m$ can be logged during training as an early caution (a small dominance gap contraindicates composing with throttling), though an automated criterion remains future work (see [Limitations] below).
5. We will state this scoping explicitly in Section 3.5 and expand the Limitations accordingly (see [Limitations] below).

**[Q2] How exactly is the dataset-level delta value computed for imbalance categorization? Is it the final-epoch EMA-smoothed gap averaged over seeds, or some statistic over the full training trajectory? Please clarify this in the main text and justify the threshold (delta > 0.15).**

**Response.** Both halves of this question deserve a precise answer.

1. **Definition.** $\delta$ is the final-epoch EMA-smoothed probe-accuracy gap measured during **baseline** training (no intervention), averaged over 5 seeds (Appendix B.9). It is not a statistic over the full trajectory. It covers the seven classification benchmarks; BraTS 2021 is a dense-prediction segmentation benchmark reported separately (Section 4.1). We will state this definition in Section 4.1 as requested.
2. **Why 0.15.** The measured values are AVE 0.017, KS 0.038, Sarcasm 0.076, Twitter15 0.150 (low group) and CMU-MOSEI 0.178, CMU-MOSI 0.222, CREMA-D 0.268 (high group). Under the paper's rule (high imbalance if $\delta$ exceeds the threshold), every threshold in $[0.150, 0.178)$ produces the identical categorization, and since $\delta$ is measured on baseline training alone, the categorization is independent of any method's results.
3. **Boundary cases.** The two datasets nearest the threshold carry the largest seed variances: Twitter15 ($0.150 \pm 0.096$) and CMU-MOSEI ($0.178 \pm 0.086$). We will state both explicitly.
4. **Sensitivity.** The per-seed spread is reported in Appendix B.9. We will add to the appendix a per-seed categorization-stability analysis, together with the categorization read at intermediate epochs and under a one-hidden-layer probe, covering the epoch, seed, and probe-quality dimensions you name. On the chosen baseline: $\delta$ is defined on the paper's standard no-intervention configuration (Section 4.1), so it inherits that recipe's architecture and optimizer choices, and we will state this dependence alongside the definition.

**[Q3] For CMU-MOSI and CMU-MOSEI, can the authors provide a deeper unimodal analysis showing whether PGGB improves the audio and visual representations even when final multimodal accuracy changes little?**

**Response.** Thank you for this suggestion, which we agree makes these datasets important extreme cases. We ran the requested analysis: post-hoc per-modality linear probes on the saved final checkpoints of all sentiment runs, mirroring the Appendix B.8 protocol (mean over 5 seeds).

**CMU-MOSEI** (probe accuracy %, baseline $\to$ PGGB boost-only): text 64.95 $\to$ 66.43, audio 51.29 $\to$ 53.17, vision 59.78 $\to$ 60.09. PGGB does improve the weaker audio representation (+1.9 pp) and vision slightly (+0.3 pp), but text also rises (+1.5 pp), and OGM-GE shows the same all-modality pattern. The post-hoc utilization gap (distinct from the training-time $\delta$ of Q2) barely moves: 13.66 $\to$ 13.26 baseline vs. PGGB, 13.04 for the composition.

**CMU-MOSI**: text 70.99 $\to$ 71.72, audio 48.86 $\to$ 49.24, vision 54.49 $\to$ 53.47. Movements are within seed noise, and the post-hoc utilization gap does not narrow (22.13 $\to$ 22.48 pp).

The contrast with CREMA-D (App. B.8, PGGB+OGM-GE composition: weak visual probe +9.41 pp, strong audio probe -0.81 pp, gap reduced $5.4\times$) is the direct answer to your question: targeted weak-modality recovery occurs where the weak modality is undertrained (trainable encoders, severe imbalance), and it is a composition effect (App. B.8 shows boost-only does not recover the CREMA-D weak probe). On the text-dominant frozen-feature pipelines the probe movements are small and non-selective (the dominant text modality rises alongside the weak ones), leaving the utilization gap essentially unchanged, consistent with weak modalities that are intrinsically limited rather than suppressed. We will add the full probe tables to the appendix and fold the undertrained-versus-intrinsically-limited distinction into Section 5 and the Limitations.

**[Q4] On text-dominant sentiment tasks, would increasing alpha help the model learn more from weaker audio/visual modalities, or does it degrade performance because those modalities are intrinsically less informative? An alpha-sensitivity study on CMU-MOSI/MOSEI would be useful.**

**Response.** Thank you for this useful suggestion. Increasing $\alpha$ neither helps nor degrades in the tested range. We ran the suggested $\alpha$-sensitivity study (boost-only, $\alpha \in \{0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.5\}$, seed 42) on both sentiment pipelines:

| Dataset | 0.25 | 0.5 | 0.75 | 1.0 | 1.5 | Range |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CMU-MOSI | 73.32 | 72.74 | 72.89 | 73.03 | 72.59 | 0.73 pp |
| CMU-MOSEI | 70.24 | 70.24 | 70.68 | 71.33 | 70.68 | 1.09 pp |

On CMU-MOSI the grid spans 0.73 pp with no monotone trend. CMU-MOSEI is mildly positive to flat (range 1.09 pp). No degradation is observed at any tested $\alpha$, so we find no evidence that stronger boosting amplifies noisy, less informative modalities into accuracy loss; these are single-seed runs, and we will report the multi-seed version in the appendix. Conversely, raising $\alpha$ also cannot unlock additional audio/visual learning here, because (per Q3) those representations are intrinsically limited rather than suppressed. We will state this scoping in the Limitations.

**[Limitations] The limitations section should more directly address high-imbalance safety, sensitivity of the imbalance categorization, and the possibility that some weak modalities may be intrinsically less task-informative rather than merely undertrained.**

**Response.** We fully agree and will expand the Limitations paragraph to cover all three items. The enhanced limitation text is presented as follows.

*Limitations.* Per-setting tuning is required, probes add small compute overhead, and composition with throttling can regress when the dominance gap is small (worst case AVE-from-scratch -4.56 pp, Section 4.3.1), a regime our theory does not cover. The three propositions are safety properties: they bound the intervention and, under Prop. 3's conditions, preserve the standard descent guarantee at any imbalance level, but they do not guarantee gap closure, which is established empirically. The two datasets nearest the imbalance threshold (Twitter15 and CMU-MOSEI) carry the largest seed variances, so their categorization is provisional. Finally, PGGB does not distinguish modalities that are undertrained from modalities that are intrinsically less task-informative. Composition with throttling recovers the former (CREMA-D), while on the latter (the sentiment benchmarks) the weak-modality representations are essentially unchanged. This diagnosis is currently post-hoc, and developing an online criterion that separates the two cases is a promising direction for future work.

We thank you again for questions that directly shaped the expanded Limitations and the new sentiment analyses, and we would welcome any follow-up during the discussion period.
