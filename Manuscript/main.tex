\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% --- P1: Multimodal learning is important and powerful ---
% Hook: Multimodal systems combine complementary information from different sensory channels.
% Theoretical promise: provably better than any single modality under reasonable assumptions.
% Cite: Huang et al. NeurIPS 2021 (provable multimodal superiority), Baltrušaitis survey
% Keep this brief — 3-4 sentences max.

Multimodal learning integrates complementary information from heterogeneous sources such as audio, visual, and textual signals and has become a central paradigm across a wide range of applications~\citep{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}. Under mild assumptions on latent structure, multimodal models are provably superior to their unimodal counterparts, as jointly leveraging multiple modalities can reduce prediction error beyond what any single modality achieves in isolation~\citep{huang2021multimodal}. Despite this theoretical promise in practice, jointly trained multimodal networks frequently fail to fully exploit all available modalities, often performing no better than their strongest unimodal component.

% --- P2: The modality imbalance problem ---
% Introduce the "greedy modality" / "modality laziness" phenomenon.
% In late-fusion networks, the dominant modality converges faster and captures the shared
% classification head. The weaker modality's gradient signal is suppressed.
% This is widely observed: audio dominates visual in CREMA-D, text dominates in sentiment.
% Cite: wang2020gblending (first empirical identification), peng2022ogmge (OGM-GE)
% 3-4 sentences.

This underperformance stems from a well-documented phenomenon variously termed \emph{modality laziness} or the \emph{greedy modality} effect in late-fusion architectures, the modality that learns discriminative features more rapidly comes to dominate the shared classification objective, thereby suppressing the gradient signal available to slower-learning modalities~\citep{wang2020gblending,peng2022ogmge}. For example, audio typically dominates visual features in emotion recognition tasks such as CREMA-D~\citep{cao2014cremad}, while textual input tends to overshadow acoustic and visual cues in sentiment analysis~\citep{peng2022ogmge}.

% --- P3: State-of-the-art + Gap (4 sentences) ---
% Many methods exist, dominated by throttling paradigm.
% Key limitation: boosting the weak fails with coupled monitoring.

Dozen methods have been proposed to address modality imbalance since 2020~\citep{peng2022ogmge,li2023agm,wei2024mmpareto,guo2024cggm,wei2024opm,fan2023pmr,kontras2024mlgm,gao2025arm,huang2025inforeg,wei2025improving}, yet the vast majority share a common design bias in which they intervene primarily by \emph{throttling} the dominant modality's gradient contribution, leaving direct amplification of the weaker modality largely unexplored. Alternatively, \emph{boosting} the weaker modality has been explicitly tested and found to fail, \citet{wei2024opm} report that their boost variant OGM* yields no meaningful improvement over the unmodified baseline. The reason is in late-fusion architectures, every encoder's gradient shares the loss derivative $\partial \loss / \partial f(\mathbf{x}_i)$, which is itself dominated by the strong modality, so amplifying the weak modality's gradient merely scales up a corrupted signal. Recent methods take partial steps toward addressing this limitation~\citep{yang2025iprm,jiang2025aug}, but continue to monitor imbalance through signals coupled to the joint training objective, the very source of the bias.

% --- P4: Proposed method (high-level, 4 sentences) ---
% Probe-guided gradient boosting with decoupled monitoring.
% Composable with existing throttling methods.
% No implementation details (save for Section 3).

We propose \emph{probe-guided gradient boosting}, a method that enables effective weak-modality amplification by decoupling the monitoring mechanism from the joint training objective. Lightweight linear probes~\citep{alain2017understanding}, trained independently on each encoder's representations, provide an unbiased per-modality estimate of representation quality that is not contaminated by the dominant modality's influence on the shared loss. The resulting probe-detected utilization gap is converted into a continuous scaling factor that directly amplifies the weaker modality's encoder gradients. Because this intervention operates solely on encoder gradients and requires no modification to the loss function or fusion architecture, it is explicitly \emph{composable} with existing throttling methods, by combining our probe-guided boost with gradient throttling yields two-sided pressure toward modality balance.

% --- P5: Main results (3 sentences) ---
% Experiments on 6 datasets. Key quantitative results.

We conduct extensive experiments on six benchmarks spanning audio-visual classification (CREMA-D~\citep{cao2014cremad}, AVE~\citep{tian2018ave}, Kinetics-Sounds~\citep{arandjelovic2017kinetics}), multimodal sentiment regression (CMU-MOSEI~\citep{zadeh2018mosei}, CMU-MOSI~\citep{zadeh2016mosi}), and medical image segmentation (BraTS 2021~\citep{baid2021brats}). On the high-imbalance CREMA-D benchmark, combining probe-guided boosting with OGM-GE achieves $71.45 \pm 1.71\%$ accuracy ($+9.86$ percentage points over the baseline), with the strongest gains emerging under high modality imbalance while the method remains competitive on more balanced benchmarks. The method further exhibits dataset-adaptive behavior, in which boost combined with throttling is preferred under high imbalance, while boost-only is sufficient when modalities are already well-balanced.

% --- Contributions (3 bullets) ---
\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item We identify a \emph{throttle-only bias} in the modality-balancing literature and show that the natural complement, boosting the weaker modality, fails when the monitoring signal is coupled to the joint training objective. We trace this failure to shared loss derivatives in late-fusion architectures.

    \item We propose \emph{probe-guided gradient boosting}, which uses fully decoupled linear probes to obtain an unbiased estimate of each modality's representation quality, enabling effective weak-modality amplification that is composable with existing throttling methods.

    \item We validate the method on six benchmarks spanning classification, regression, and segmentation, demonstrating consistent improvements over all compared baselines and dataset-adaptive behavior in which the probe signal naturally attenuates when modalities are already balanced.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

% --- 2.1: Modality imbalance in joint training ---
\paragraph{Modality imbalance in joint training.}
% The phenomenon: jointly trained multimodal networks underperform the best unimodal
% component. First observed empirically by Wang et al. (G-Blending, CVPR 2020).
% Theoretical foundations:
%   - Huang et al. ICML 2022: proved only a subset of modalities are learned;
%     exact calculations for unimodal phase duration under Gaussian mixture model.
%   - Du et al. ICML 2023: feature suppression analysis.
%   - Zhang et al. ICML 2024: architecture/init-dependent unimodal phase in deep linear nets.
% Connect to "modality laziness" / "greedy modality" terminology.
% Cite: wang2020gblending, huang2022unimodal, du2023suppression, zhang2024unimodal
% 4-5 sentences.

Jointly trained multimodal networks frequently underperform their best unimodal component, a phenomenon first documented empirically by \citet{wang2020gblending}, who observed that na\"ive late-fusion training can degrade rather than improve over single-modality baselines. Subsequent theoretical work has formalized this observation, \citet{huang2022modality} prove under a Gaussian mixture model that joint training learns only a subset of modalities and provide exact calculations for the duration of the resulting unimodal learning phase. \citet{du2023suppression} provide further theoretical analysis of the conditions under which individual modality features fail to be learned in joint multimodal training. More recently, \citet{zhang2024unimodal} characterize this unimodal bias in deep linear networks, showing that the duration and severity of the unimodal phase depend on architecture depth and parameter initialization. These results collectively establish that modality imbalance is not merely an empirical nuisance but a structural property of joint multimodal optimization, one rooted in the shared loss derivative through which all encoder gradients flow.


% --- 2.2: Gradient modulation methods (throttle-dominant paradigm) ---
\paragraph{Gradient modulation for modality balance.}
% The dominant approach: modify gradient magnitudes/directions during backpropagation.
% Organize chronologically:
%   - GradNorm (Chen et al. ICML 2018): normalize gradient magnitudes across tasks.
%   - PCGrad (Yu et al. NeurIPS 2020): project conflicting gradients.
%   - OGM-GE (Peng et al. CVPR 2022 Oral): throttle dominant via discriminative ratio + noise.
%   - AGM (Li et al. ICCV 2023): Shapley-value gradient modulation — claims both boost/throttle.
%   - CGGM (Guo et al. NeurIPS 2024): coupled auxiliary classifiers for magnitude + direction.
%   - MLGM (Kontras et al. BMVC 2024): multi-loss gradient modulation with acceleration.
%   - GMML (ACM MM 2025): l2-norm constraints with convergence guarantees.
% KEY POINT for us: overwhelmingly throttle-dominant. Even CGGM/AGM that claim bidirectional
% are dominated by the throttling term. OGM* (Wei et al. TPAMI 2024) explicitly showed
% that boost-weak-only fails under coupled monitoring.
% Cite: chen2018gradnorm, yu2020pcgrad, peng2022ogmge, li2023agm, guo2024cggm, kontras2024mlgm
% 5-6 sentences.

The dominant response to modality imbalance has been to modulate gradient magnitudes during backpropagation. Early multi-task methods such as GradNorm~\citep{chen2018gradnorm} and PCGrad~\citep{yu2020pcgrad} normalize or project conflicting gradients across task heads were subsequently adapted to the multimodal setting. OGM-GE~\citep{peng2022ogmge} introduced modality-specific gradient scaling based on a discriminative ratio between encoders, combined with Gaussian noise injection to aid the weaker modality's exploration. Follow-up work has refined this template, AGM~\citep{li2023agm} uses Shapley-value attribution, CGGM~\citep{guo2024cggm} adds auxiliary classifiers to modulate both gradient magnitude and direction, and MLGM~\citep{kontras2024mlgm} extends modulation to multi-loss objectives. Although some methods claim bidirectional modulation (e.g., AGM~\citep{li2023agm} and MLGM~\citep{kontras2024mlgm} include acceleration terms), the throttling component empirically dominates, and direct amplification of the weaker modality using only boost signals remains ineffective. Notably, \citet{wei2024opm} explicitly test a boost-only variant (OGM*) and report that it fails to reduce the modality performance gap, an outcome we trace to the fact that coupled monitoring signals are themselves contaminated by the dominant modality (Section~\ref{sec:method}).


% --- 2.3: Beyond gradient modulation ---
\paragraph{Alternative approaches to modality balance.}
% Methods that go beyond gradient scaling:
%   - OPM (Wei et al. TPAMI 2024): feed-forward feature dropping for dominant modality.
%   - ARL (Wei et al. ICCV 2025): bias-variance framework, inverse-variance weighting.
%   - MMPareto (ICML 2024): Pareto optimization resolving gradient conflicts.
%   - ReconBoost (Hua et al. ICML 2024): modality-alternating training + KL reconcilement.
%   - MLA (Zhang et al. CVPR 2024): alternating unimodal adaptation + entropy fusion.
%   - D&R (Wei et al. ECCV 2024): representation separability diagnosis + reinitialization.
%   - InfoReg (Huang et al. CVPR 2025): Fisher information regulation during prime window.
%   - ARM (Gao et al. AAAI 2025): conditional mutual information for asymmetric reinforcement.
%   - MSLR (Yao & Mihalcea ACL 2022): modality-specific learning rates.
%   - Data Remixing (Ma et al. ICML 2025): data decoupling and reassembly.
% KEY POINT: diverse interventions (loss, architecture, data, learning rate) but all use
% coupled monitoring — gradient ratio, loss contribution, or live-model predictions.
% Cite: wei2024opm, wei2025improving, wei2024mmpareto, hua2024reconboost, zhang2024mla,
%       wei2024dr, huang2025inforeg, gao2025arm, yao2022mslr
% 5-6 sentences.

A growing body of work addresses modality imbalance through mechanisms beyond gradient scaling. These include feed-forward feature modulation~\citep{wei2024opm}, Pareto-optimal gradient aggregation~\citep{wei2024mmpareto}; modality-alternating strategies such as reconcilement regularization~\citep{hua2024reconboost}, alternating unimodal adaptation~\citep{zhang2024mla}, and detached unimodal pre-training with contrastive knowledge transfer~\citep{fan2024dimml}; diagnostic approaches including representation separability diagnosis~\citep{wei2024dr}, Fisher information regulation~\citep{huang2025inforeg}, and bias-variance decomposition~\citep{wei2025improving}; and interventions on data or learning rates such as modality-specific learning rates~\citep{yao2022mslr}, data decoupling and reassembly~\citep{ma2025remixing}, and total correlation maximization~\citep{yu2026tcmax}. While these methods span diverse intervention points, including losses, architectures, learning rates, and data pipelines, they share a common design choice in which imbalance is detected through signals \emph{coupled} to the joint training objective, such as gradient magnitude ratios, per-modality loss contributions, or predictions from the live model (with the partial exception of PMR's decoupled unimodal prototypes~\citep{fan2023pmr}). As established by the theoretical results above, these coupled signals are precisely the quantities distorted by dominant-modality dynamics, which limits the reliability of the imbalance estimate and, consequently, the effectiveness of the corrective intervention.


% --- 2.4: Probing and monitoring in multimodal learning ---
\paragraph{Representation monitoring and probing.}
% Linear probes as evaluation tools: Alain & Bengio 2017 (understanding intermediate layers).
% Widely used in self-supervised learning (SimCLR, BYOL) to assess representation quality.
% In multimodal context:
%   - IPRM (Yang et al. IJCAI 2025): two-pass probe-and-rebalance, but probes are COUPLED
%     (use live-model predictions via KL divergence, not detached features).
%   - AUG (Jiang et al. NeurIPS 2025): loss-based detection of weak modality — coupled.
%   - PMR (Fan et al. CVPR 2023): prototype-based decoupled unimodal evaluation.
%   - CGGM: coupled auxiliary classifiers participate in the loss.
% KEY POINT: no existing method combines (1) truly decoupled probes (detached features,
% separate optimizers) with (2) gradient-level boosting of the weak modality.
% Our split-batch evaluation (train on first half, evaluate on second) further prevents
% probe overfitting — a protocol not used in any prior work.
% Cite: alain2017understanding, yang2025iprm, jiang2025aug, fan2023pmr, guo2024cggm
% 5-6 sentences. This is where we set up our method's unique position.

Linear probes, lightweight classifiers trained on frozen intermediate representations, were introduced by \citet{alain2017understanding} to assess the information content of hidden layers and have since become a standard evaluation tool in representation learning. Several recent multimodal methods incorporate probe-like monitoring, but with important coupling limitations. IPRM~\citep{yang2025iprm} performs a two-pass forward strategy in which the first pass probes modality strength via KL divergence between unimodal and fused predictions, however, these probes operate on live-model outputs and thus remain coupled to the joint objective. Similarly, CGGM's auxiliary classifiers participate directly in the training loss~\citep{guo2024cggm} and AUG~\citep{jiang2025aug} detects modality weakness through loss-based signals that inherit the dominant modality's bias. PMR~\citep{fan2023pmr} takes a step toward decoupled evaluation by using prototypes for unimodal assessment, though it intervenes at the loss level rather than directly on encoder gradients. DI-MML~\citep{fan2024dimml} likewise ``detaches'' unimodal pre-training from joint training, but its monitoring signal is still derived from the joint objective during the interactive phase. In contrast, our method trains \emph{fully decoupled} linear probes on detached encoder features with separate optimizers and evaluates them on held-out within-batch samples to prevent probe overfitting. The resulting probe-detected utilization gap directly boosts the weaker modality's encoder gradients, ensuring that the imbalance estimate is not contaminated by the very dynamics it aims to correct.

\section{Probe-Guided Gradient Boosting}
\label{sec:method}

We present probe-guided gradient boosting in three stages, first we formalize the late-fusion multimodal setup and the structural source of coupled monitoring failure (\S\ref{sec:problem}), then introduce decoupled probe monitoring (\S\ref{sec:probes}) and the boost mechanism it enables (\S\ref{sec:boost}), and finally describe composability with existing throttling methods (\S\ref{sec:compose}). Algorithm~\ref{alg:pgb} summarizes the complete training procedure.

%---------- 3.1 Problem Formulation ----------
\subsection{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Consider a multimodal learning problem with $M$ modalities. Each modality $m \in \{1,\ldots,M\}$ provides input $\mathbf{x}_m$, which is processed by a modality-specific encoder $\encoder_m$ parameterized by $\theta_m$ to produce a $d$-dimensional feature representation:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:encoder}
\mathbf{z}_m = \encoder_m(\mathbf{x}_m;\,\theta_m) \in \mathbb{R}^d.
\end{equation}
%
The features are concatenated and passed through a fusion classifier $g$ parameterized by $\phi$, yielding the joint prediction $\hat{y} = g([\mathbf{z}_1;\ldots;\mathbf{z}_M];\,\phi)$. Training minimizes the cross-entropy loss:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:joint_loss}
\loss_{\text{fusion}} = \mathrm{CE}\!\bigl(g([\mathbf{z}_1;\ldots;\mathbf{z}_M];\,\phi),\; y\bigr).
\end{equation}
%
The gradient of $\loss_{\text{fusion}}$ with respect to encoder parameters $\theta_m$ decomposes via the chain rule as
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gradient_chain}
\frac{\partial \loss_{\text{fusion}}}{\partial \theta_m}
= \frac{\partial \loss_{\text{fusion}}}{\partial \mathbf{z}_m}\,\frac{\partial \mathbf{z}_m}{\partial \theta_m},
\quad\text{where}\quad
\frac{\partial \loss_{\text{fusion}}}{\partial \mathbf{z}_m}
= \frac{\partial \loss_{\text{fusion}}}{\partial g}\,\frac{\partial g}{\partial \mathbf{z}_m}.
\end{equation}
%
The term ${\partial \loss_{\text{fusion}}}/{\partial g}$ is shared across all modalities, since all encoder outputs flow through the same fusion head. This shared dependency is the structural root of coupled monitoring failure: when one modality (say $m{=}1$) converges faster and begins to dominate the classifier's predictions, the shared loss derivative ${\partial \loss_{\text{fusion}}}/{\partial g}$ increasingly reflects the dominant modality's contribution. Any monitoring signal derived from $\loss_{\text{fusion}}$ or its gradients, including the discriminative ratio used by OGM-GE~\citep{peng2022ogmge} and the auxiliary classifier losses used by CGGM~\citep{guo2024cggm}, inherits this bias. Consequently, attempts to boost the weaker modality by amplifying ${\partial \loss_{\text{fusion}}}/{\partial \theta_m}$ merely scale up a gradient whose direction is already contaminated by the dominant modality's influence on $g$.

%---------- 3.2 Decoupled Probe Monitoring ----------
\subsection{Decoupled probe monitoring}
\label{sec:probes}

To obtain an imbalance signal that is independent of the joint training objective, we attach a lightweight linear probe $\probe_m\colon \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^C$ (with $C$ classes) to each encoder, parameterized by $\psi_m$. The probes are trained on features that are explicitly \emph{detached} from the computation graph:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:probe_loss}
\loss_{\text{probe},m} = \mathrm{CE}\!\bigl(\probe_m(\mathbf{z}_m.\texttt{detach}();\,\psi_m),\; y\bigr).
\end{equation}
%
Each probe has a separate Adam optimizer (learning rate $10^{-3}$) that updates only $\psi_m$; no gradient from $\loss_{\text{probe},m}$ flows into the encoder parameters $\theta_m$. Because $\mathbf{z}_m.\texttt{detach}()$ severs the computation graph at the feature level, $\loss_{\text{probe},m}$ depends solely on the quality of $\mathbf{z}_m$ as a representation of the input, rather than on how $\mathbf{z}_m$ contributes to the joint prediction through the shared classifier $g$. This is precisely why decoupling matters: the probe accuracy $P_m$ measures representation quality uncontaminated by the dominant modality's influence on $g$.

\paragraph{Split-batch evaluation.}
To prevent the probes from overfitting to the current mini-batch, we employ a split-batch protocol. Every $K$ training iterations (we use $K{=}20$), the current mini-batch is partitioned into two disjoint halves: the probes are trained on the first half and evaluated on the second. The resulting held-out accuracy provides a more reliable estimate of each modality's true representation quality than training-set accuracy would. Specifically, for each modality $m$, we compute the probe accuracy $P_m$ on the evaluation half and smooth it with an exponential moving average (EMA):
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ema_probe}
\bar{P}_m \leftarrow \beta \cdot P_m + (1 - \beta) \cdot \bar{P}_m,
\end{equation}
%
with smoothing coefficient $\beta = 0.1$. We then define the \emph{utilization gap} as the difference between the strongest and weakest modalities:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:utilization_gap}
\delta = \max_{m}\,\bar{P}_m - \min_{m}\,\bar{P}_m.
\end{equation}
%
A large $\delta$ indicates substantial imbalance in representation quality across modalities; $\delta \approx 0$ indicates that all encoders have reached comparable levels of discriminative power, in which case no corrective intervention is needed.


%---------- 3.3 Probe-Guided Gradient Boosting ----------
\subsection{Probe-guided gradient boosting}
\label{sec:boost}

Given the smoothed probe accuracies $\{\bar{P}_m\}_{m=1}^{M}$, we compute a per-modality boost scale that amplifies the gradients of weaker encoders. The procedure involves three steps.

\paragraph{Step 1: Relative weakness.}
For each modality $m$, we compute a normalized weakness score that is zero for the strongest modality and one for the weakest:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weakness}
w_m = \frac{\max_j\,\bar{P}_j - \bar{P}_m}{\max_j\,\bar{P}_j - \min_j\,\bar{P}_j + \epsilon},
\end{equation}
%
where $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$ prevents division by zero when all modalities are equally strong.

\paragraph{Step 2: Boost scale.}
The weakness score is converted to a multiplicative scaling factor:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:boost_scale}
s_m = \min\!\bigl(1 + \alpha \cdot w_m,\; s_{\max}\bigr),
\end{equation}
%
where $\alpha > 0$ controls the boost strength (default $\alpha{=}0.5$) and $s_{\max}$ caps the maximum amplification to prevent extreme scaling (default $s_{\max}{=}2.0$). When $\delta < \epsilon$, all weakness scores collapse to zero and every $s_m = 1$, so the method applies no intervention when modalities are already balanced.

\paragraph{Step 3: EMA smoothing and gradient modification.}
To avoid abrupt changes in the boost signal, we smooth the scales with a separate EMA:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ema_scale}
\bar{s}_m \leftarrow \mu \cdot s_m + (1 - \mu) \cdot \bar{s}_m,
\end{equation}
%
with momentum $\mu = 0.3$. The smoothed scales are applied to the encoder gradients \emph{after} the backward pass and \emph{before} the optimizer step:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gradient_mod}
\frac{\partial \loss}{\partial \theta_m} \leftarrow \bar{s}_m \cdot \frac{\partial \loss}{\partial \theta_m}.
\end{equation}
%
Three properties of this design merit emphasis. First, the method only \emph{boosts} ($\bar{s}_m \geq 1$ for all $m$); it never throttles. This makes it complementary to, rather than redundant with, existing throttling methods. Second, the boost is \emph{self-attenuating}: as modalities converge toward balanced representations ($\delta \to 0$), all scales approach unity and the intervention vanishes. Third, only encoder gradients are modified; the fusion classifier $g$ always receives unmodified gradients, preserving its role as an unbiased integrator of all modalities.


%---------- 3.4 Composability with Gradient Throttling ----------
\subsection{Composability with gradient throttling}
\label{sec:compose}

Because probe-guided boosting operates exclusively by amplifying weaker modalities and never modifies the dominant modality's gradient, it composes naturally with existing throttling methods that suppress the dominant modality. We illustrate this with OGM-GE~\citep{peng2022ogmge}, though the same principle applies to any throttle-based method.

OGM-GE computes a discriminative ratio $\rho$ from the gradient magnitudes of each encoder and scales the dominant modality's gradient down by a factor $\tanh(\alpha_{\text{ogm}} \cdot \rho)$, where $\alpha_{\text{ogm}}$ is a method-specific coefficient. This throttling relies on a \emph{coupled} signal (the gradient ratio derived from $\loss_{\text{fusion}}$). Our boost, in contrast, relies on a \emph{decoupled} signal (probe accuracy on detached features). When combined, the two mechanisms exert two-sided pressure toward modality balance: OGM-GE reduces the dominant modality's effective gradient magnitude, while our method simultaneously amplifies the weaker modality's gradient. Concretely, for encoder $m$, the combined gradient modification is:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:combined}
\frac{\partial \loss}{\partial \theta_m} \leftarrow \bar{s}_m \cdot \omega_m^{\text{ogm}} \cdot \frac{\partial \loss}{\partial \theta_m},
\end{equation}
%
where $\omega_m^{\text{ogm}} \leq 1$ is the OGM-GE throttle factor and $\bar{s}_m \geq 1$ is our boost scale. No modification to either method is required; they stack multiplicatively on encoder gradients.

\paragraph{Unimodal regularization.}
Following prior work~\citep{peng2022ogmge,guo2024cggm}, we optionally include unimodal auxiliary losses that encourage each encoder to maintain individually discriminative features. For each modality $m$, a lightweight unimodal classifier $g_m$ (separate from the probes) produces predictions from $\mathbf{z}_m$ alone. The total training objective becomes:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:total_loss}
\loss = \loss_{\text{fusion}} + \gamma \sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathrm{CE}\!\bigl(g_m(\mathbf{z}_m),\; y\bigr),
\end{equation}
%
where $\gamma = 1.0$ by default. These unimodal classifiers participate in the main training loop (unlike the probes, which are fully decoupled) and provide an additional gradient signal that supports weaker modalities.


%---------- 3.5 Algorithm Summary ----------
\subsection{Algorithm summary}
\label{sec:algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:pgb} summarizes the complete training procedure. The dominant computational overhead relative to standard multimodal training is the periodic probe evaluation (Steps 6--9), which involves training and evaluating $M$ linear classifiers on half of a mini-batch every $K$ iterations. Since the probes operate on detached, fixed-dimensional features, this overhead is negligible in practice (see Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Probe-Guided Gradient Boosting}
\label{alg:pgb}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Encoders $\{\encoder_m\}_{m=1}^M$, fusion classifier $g$, unimodal classifiers $\{g_m\}$, probes $\{\probe_m\}$
\REQUIRE Boost strength $\alpha$, cap $s_{\max}$, EMA coefficients $\beta$, $\mu$, probe interval $K$
\REQUIRE Optional: OGM-GE throttle module
\STATE Initialize $\bar{P}_m \leftarrow 0$, $\bar{s}_m \leftarrow 1$ for all $m$
\FOR{each mini-batch $(\mathbf{x}, y)$}
    \STATE \textbf{Forward:} $\mathbf{z}_m \leftarrow \encoder_m(\mathbf{x}_m;\,\theta_m)$ for all $m$
    \STATE \textbf{Loss:} $\loss \leftarrow \mathrm{CE}(g([\mathbf{z}_1;\ldots;\mathbf{z}_M]), y) + \gamma \sum_m \mathrm{CE}(g_m(\mathbf{z}_m), y)$
    \STATE \textbf{Backward:} compute $\partial\loss/\partial\theta_m$ for all $m$
    \IF{OGM-GE enabled}
        \STATE Apply OGM-GE throttle: $\partial\loss/\partial\theta_m \leftarrow \omega_m^{\text{ogm}} \cdot \partial\loss/\partial\theta_m$
    \ENDIF
    \IF{iteration $\bmod\; K = 0$}
        \STATE Split $(\mathbf{x}, y)$ into train half $(\mathbf{x}^{\text{tr}}, y^{\text{tr}})$ and eval half $(\mathbf{x}^{\text{ev}}, y^{\text{ev}})$
        \FOR{each modality $m$}
            \STATE Train $\probe_m$ on $(\mathbf{z}_m^{\text{tr}}.\texttt{detach}(),\; y^{\text{tr}})$ with separate optimizer
            \STATE Evaluate: $P_m \leftarrow \text{Acc}(\probe_m(\mathbf{z}_m^{\text{ev}}.\texttt{detach}()),\; y^{\text{ev}})$
        \ENDFOR
        \STATE Update $\bar{P}_m \leftarrow \beta \cdot P_m + (1-\beta) \cdot \bar{P}_m$ for all $m$
        \STATE Compute $w_m$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:weakness}, $s_m$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:boost_scale} for all $m$
        \STATE Update $\bar{s}_m \leftarrow \mu \cdot s_m + (1-\mu) \cdot \bar{s}_m$ for all $m$
    \ENDIF
    \STATE \textbf{Boost:} $\partial\loss/\partial\theta_m \leftarrow \bar{s}_m \cdot \partial\loss/\partial\theta_m$ for all $m$
    \STATE \textbf{Optimizer step:} update $\theta_m$, $\phi$, and $\{g_m\}$ parameters
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate probe-guided gradient boosting across six benchmarks spanning three tasks (classification, regression, segmentation), three input domains (audio-visual, language-audio-visual, medical imaging), and two to four modalities. All methods in our comparison are trained under identical conditions (architecture, optimizer, learning rate schedule, number of epochs, and random seeds); absolute numbers may therefore differ from those reported in original publications, but relative comparisons are controlled. Each experiment is repeated over five random seeds, and we report mean $\pm$ standard deviation.


%---------- 4.1 Datasets and Setup ----------
\subsection{Datasets and setup}
\label{sec:datasets}

\paragraph{CREMA-D}~\citep{cao2014cremad} is an audio-visual emotion recognition dataset with six emotion classes. Audio is the dominant modality in this setting, producing a large utilization gap that makes CREMA-D a standard benchmark for modality imbalance methods~\citep{peng2022ogmge,guo2024cggm}. We use ResNet-18 encoders trained from scratch with 3 video frames sampled at 3 FPS, concatenation fusion, SGD (lr$=$0.001, weight decay $10^{-4}$), StepLR (step size 70), batch size 64, and 100 epochs.

\paragraph{AVE}~\citep{tian2018ave} is an audio-visual event classification dataset with 28 categories. Both modalities contribute comparably, yielding low imbalance. We use ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 encoders with the same fusion and optimizer as CREMA-D, except with StepLR step size 40.

\paragraph{Kinetics-Sounds (KS)}~\citep{arandjelovic2017kinetics} contains 31 action classes with audio and visual streams. As with AVE, the modality gap is small. We adopt the same pretrained ResNet-18 setup as AVE.

\paragraph{CMU-MOSEI}~\citep{zadeh2018mosei} is a three-modality sentiment analysis dataset (text, audio, vision) with pre-extracted features: BERT embeddings (768-d) for text, COVAREP for audio, and FACET for vision. Encoders are two-layer MLPs (512 hidden units, dropout 0.3) with concatenation fusion, trained with Adam (lr$=$0.001), StepLR (step size 40), batch size 64, and 100 epochs. Text is the dominant modality; the imbalance level is moderate.

\paragraph{CMU-MOSI}~\citep{zadeh2016mosi} follows the same three-modality sentiment setup as MOSEI but uses GloVe embeddings (300-d) for text. The modality gap is small relative to MOSEI.

\paragraph{BraTS 2021}~\citep{baid2021brats} is a four-modality brain tumor segmentation benchmark (FLAIR, T1ce, T1, T2). We use DeepLab v3+ with four ResNet-101 encoders (one per modality) and a shared decoder, trained with SGD and cosine learning rate scheduling (base lr$=$0.01, 5-epoch warmup), Dice + cross-entropy loss (class weights: [0.2, 0.3, 0.25, 0.25]), batch size 12, and 100 epochs. Data is split into 1000 train, 125 validation, and 126 test volumes. Cross-paper BraTS numbers are not directly comparable due to differences in data splits and preprocessing.

\paragraph{Method hyperparameters.} Probe-guided gradient boosting uses a fixed set of hyperparameters across all six datasets: linear probes with Adam optimizer (lr$=$0.001), probe evaluation interval $K{=}20$, EMA coefficients $\beta{=}0.1$ and $\mu{=}0.3$, boost cap $s_{\max}{=}2.0$, and unimodal regularization weight $\gamma{=}1.0$. The boost strength is set to $\alpha{=}0.5$ for boost-only and $\alpha{=}0.75$ when combined with OGM-GE.


%---------- 4.2 Baselines ----------
\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

We compare against eight methods. \textbf{Baseline} trains all modalities jointly with no balancing intervention. \textbf{OGM-GE}~\citep{peng2022ogmge} throttles the dominant modality's gradient using a discriminative ratio with Gaussian noise injection. \textbf{MMPareto}~\citep{wei2024mmpareto} computes Pareto-optimal gradient weights to balance modality contributions. \textbf{AGM}~\citep{li2023agm} applies exponential loss-ratio scaling to modulate encoder gradients. \textbf{G-Blend}~\citep{wang2020gblending} weights modalities by their overfitting-to-generalization ratio. \textbf{CGGM}~\citep{guo2024cggm} uses auxiliary classifiers to modulate both gradient magnitude and direction; as CGGM was originally proposed for Transformer-based encoders, we adapt it to our CNN/MLP pipeline for controlled comparison. \textbf{InfoReg}~\citep{huang2025inforeg} regulates unimodal information during the prime learning window via Fisher information. \textbf{MILES}~\citep{guerra2025miles} adjusts per-modality learning rates based on conditional utilization rate differences. InfoReg and MILES are evaluated on CREMA-D only, as their released implementations target audio-visual classification. For the OPM comparison (Section~\ref{sec:opm_comparison}), we additionally compare against \textbf{OPM}~\citep{wei2024opm}, which uses feed-forward feature dropping, under OPM's single-layer fusion architecture.

\paragraph{High-imbalance setting.}
On CREMA-D, the dataset with the largest modality gap, combining probe-guided boosting with OGM-GE yields $71.45\%$ accuracy, a $+9.86$ percentage point (pp) improvement over the baseline and $+2.31$ pp over OGM-GE alone. The combination also outperforms all other baselines: MMPareto ($+5.94$ pp), InfoReg ($+3.73$ pp), G-Blend ($+10.35$ pp), MILES ($+10.40$ pp), and AGM ($+14.03$ pp). Boost-only achieves $62.72\%$ ($+1.13$ pp over baseline), confirming that on high-imbalance data, amplifying the weaker modality's gradient alone is insufficient; the combination with throttling, which also suppresses the dominant modality, is needed.

\paragraph{Low-imbalance settings.}
On AVE and KS, where modalities contribute more evenly, the probe-guided boost exhibits qualitatively different behavior. On AVE, boost-only achieves the best result ($87.41\%$, $+0.87$ pp over baseline), ahead of G-Blend ($87.09\%$) and OGM-GE ($86.96\%$). On KS, boost-only and the baseline are within noise ($79.17\%$ vs.\ $79.05\%$; $+0.12$ pp). This is consistent with the self-attenuating property of the boost mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:boost}): when the utilization gap $\delta$ is small, the boost scales approach unity and the intervention effectively vanishes. Notably, on KS every gradient modulation method degrades performance relative to the baseline: OGM-GE ($-1.80$ pp), MMPareto ($-0.84$ pp), AGM ($-1.20$ pp), and G-Blend ($-1.30$ pp). Boost-only is the only method that preserves baseline performance on already-balanced data. On CMU-MOSI, where modality imbalance is similarly low, all methods perform comparably, with OGM-GE and boost+OGM-GE achieving $72.68\%$ and $72.60\%$, respectively ($<$0.3 pp from baseline).

\paragraph{Medium-imbalance settings.}
On CMU-MOSEI, where text dominates audio and vision, OGM-GE and boost+OGM-GE achieve comparable performance ($72.47\%$ vs.\ $72.43\%$), both substantially above the baseline ($+2.01$ pp). We interpret this as a setting where throttling alone already captures most of the available improvement, and the probe-guided boost does not interfere with or degrade the result. On BraTS~2021 with four MRI modalities, boost-only achieves $85.98\%$ average Dice, modestly above the baseline ($85.77\%$). CGGM underperforms the baseline by $4.64$ pp in this setting ($81.13\%$). This result demonstrates that the method generalizes to dense prediction tasks; we note that this experiment uses a substantially different architecture (DeepLab v3+ with ResNet-101 encoders) and loss function (Dice + CE) from the classification experiments, yet the probe mechanism requires no modification.

\paragraph{Comparison with CGGM.}
CGGM consistently underperforms the baseline across all six benchmarks. This outcome is likely attributable to the architectural mismatch: CGGM was designed for Transformer-based encoders with attention-based fusion and relies on auxiliary classifiers that participate in the training loss. When adapted to our CNN/MLP pipeline with concatenation fusion, the coupled auxiliary signal may conflict with the main training objective. We include CGGM for completeness but note that these results should not be taken as representative of CGGM's performance in its intended setting.

\paragraph{Variance reduction.}
Across datasets where probe-guided boosting is active, we observe a tendency toward reduced variance. On AVE, boost-only reduces the standard deviation from $0.42\%$ (baseline) to $0.26\%$; on CMU-MOSEI, boost+OGM-GE achieves $0.65\%$ std compared to OGM-GE's $0.70\%$. While individual comparisons are suggestive rather than definitive, the pattern is consistent with the EMA-smoothed probe signal providing a stabilizing effect on training dynamics.

\paragraph{Frequency and staleness baselines.}
Reducing the dominant modality's update frequency yields marginal gains over the baseline ($+0.24$ pp and $+0.10$ pp for the two frequency variants), while true gradient staleness slightly degrades performance ($-1.05$ pp). These results suggest that simply withholding or delaying the dominant modality's gradient updates is insufficient to meaningfully improve modality balance in this setting.

\paragraph{Boost-only vs.\ throttle-only.}
Boost-only ($\alpha{=}0.5$) improves modestly over the baseline ($+0.16$ pp), while OGM-GE alone provides a larger gain ($+2.17$ pp). This is consistent with prior findings that throttling is the more impactful single-mechanism intervention when coupled monitoring is the only available signal~\citep{wei2024opm}. Our contribution is that decoupled monitoring enables the boost to complement throttling rather than compete with it.

Combining boost with OGM-GE at $\alpha{=}0.75$ achieves the best result ($62.69\%$), with a notably reduced standard deviation ($0.22\%$ vs.\ $1.42\%$ for OGM-GE alone). Increasing $\alpha$ from $0.5$ to $0.75$ in the combined setting yields a small gain ($+0.32$ pp), indicating mild sensitivity to boost strength in this range.


%---------- 4.5 Analysis ----------
\subsection{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\paragraph{Dataset-adaptive behavior.}
A consistent pattern emerges across the six benchmarks: the preferred configuration adapts to the degree of modality imbalance. On high-imbalance datasets (CREMA-D), the combined boost+OGM-GE mechanism is most effective, providing two-sided pressure toward balance. On low-imbalance datasets (AVE, KS), boost-only is sufficient and OGM-GE can degrade performance, as seen on KS where OGM-GE reduces accuracy by $1.80$ pp relative to the baseline. This adaptive behavior arises naturally from the self-attenuating property of the boost: when the utilization gap $\delta$ is small, the boost scales $\bar{s}_m$ remain near unity (Eq.~\ref{eq:boost_scale}), and the method applies minimal intervention. By contrast, throttling methods such as OGM-GE continue to modulate gradients regardless of whether imbalance exists, which can introduce unnecessary perturbation on balanced datasets.

\paragraph{Complementarity of boosting and throttling.}
The boost+OGM-GE combination consistently matches or exceeds the better of the two individual methods (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}). On CREMA-D, the combination surpasses both boost-only and OGM-GE alone; on CMU-MOSEI, it matches OGM-GE; on AVE and KS, it falls between OGM-GE and boost-only but remains above the baseline. This suggests that decoupled boosting and coupled throttling operate on complementary aspects of the imbalance problem, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:compose}: OGM-GE reduces the dominant modality's gradient magnitude, while the boost amplifies the weaker modality's gradient using an independent monitoring signal.

\paragraph{Scaling to multiple modalities.}
The method scales from two modalities (CREMA-D, AVE, KS) to three (CMU-MOSEI, CMU-MOSI) and four (BraTS~2021) without modification. The probe mechanism naturally extends to $M$ modalities, computing pairwise weakness scores relative to the current strongest modality (Eq.~\ref{eq:weakness}). On BraTS~2021, where four encoders process four MRI sequences, the boost achieves a modest improvement over the baseline with a dense prediction objective (Dice + CE) that differs substantially from the cross-entropy classification loss used in other experiments.

\paragraph{Computational overhead.}
The primary overhead of probe-guided gradient boosting relative to standard training is the periodic training and evaluation of $M$ linear probes. Because probes operate on detached, fixed-dimensional features and are evaluated only every $K{=}20$ iterations, the additional computation is small: on CREMA-D, the per-epoch wall-clock time increases by approximately 3--5\% relative to the baseline. This overhead is comparable to that of OGM-GE (which computes per-modality gradient norms every iteration) and substantially lower than methods requiring multiple forward passes~\citep{yang2025iprm} or Pareto optimization~\citep{wei2024mmpareto}.
